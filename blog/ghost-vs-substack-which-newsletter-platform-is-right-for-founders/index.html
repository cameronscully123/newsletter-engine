Ghost vs Substack: Which Newsletter Platform Is Right for Founders?

At some point, every founder building a newsletter ends up comparing Ghost and Substack. They are the two platforms that keep coming up in conversations about owning your audience, and both have genuine reasons to consider them.

But they are not really competing for the same thing. Ghost and Substack were built on different assumptions about what a newsletter publisher needs, and understanding that difference makes the choice much more straightforward than the endless comparison articles suggest.

What Each Platform Was Built For

Substack started as a way to help writers monetise through paid subscriptions. The product is simple by design. You write, you publish, you grow. The platform takes 10% of your subscription revenue in exchange for handling everything else — hosting, payments, discovery, comments, and community features.

Ghost is a different beast. It is open-source software built around the idea of owning your platform entirely. You can host Ghost yourself for free, or pay for Ghost's managed hosting. Either way, you keep full control over your design, your data, your domain, and how you structure your membership. Ghost takes nothing from your subscription revenue.

That foundational difference shapes every other comparison between the two.

The Pricing Reality at Scale

For founders who are just starting out and have no paying subscribers yet, Substack is genuinely free. You only pay Substack's 10% cut when your readers start paying. This is a meaningful advantage early on — there is no platform fee sitting between you and your first 100 subscribers.

Ghost's managed hosting starts at around $9 per month on the Starter plan and goes up from there depending on your needs. If you are not generating revenue yet, that is a real monthly cost.

The calculus changes as your paid subscriber list grows. A newsletter earning $2,000 a month in subscription revenue hands Substack $200 every month. At $5,000 a month, that is $500 going to the platform. Ghost at any paid tier costs a fraction of that, regardless of how much subscription revenue you generate.

For founders building toward a paid newsletter as a genuine revenue stream, the maths eventually swing firmly in Ghost's favour.

Where Substack Has a Clear Edge: Discovery

Substack built a network. Notes, Recommendations, and the Substack reader app all create internal mechanics that can bring you new subscribers without you doing anything extra. Some newsletters report 30% to 40% of new subscribers coming through Substack's own recommendation engine.

This is not trivial. If you are early-stage and do not yet have a reliable external growth channel, Substack's built-in discoverability can genuinely move the needle on list growth.

Ghost has no equivalent discovery layer. Every subscriber you get on Ghost, you have to earn through your own marketing, social presence, or SEO. That is fine once you have momentum. Before you have momentum, it requires more deliberate effort.

Where Ghost Has a Clear Edge: Ownership and Control

Ghost gives you your data, your design, and your domain in a way that Substack does not. Your subscriber list on Ghost is yours without restriction. Your content lives on a URL you control. You can build a proper website with category pages, a searchable archive, custom landing pages, and a brand identity that looks like a real publication.

For founders who care about SEO, Ghost is meaningfully stronger. Content published on Ghost can rank in search results like any standard website. Substack content can rank too, but the structural control Ghost gives you over internal linking, metadata, and page architecture makes it a more capable SEO asset over time.

If your strategy involves building a content library that compounds over years — not just a weekly email that gets sent and archived — Ghost is the more capable platform.

Which One to Choose

If you are starting from zero, have no paying subscribers yet, and are not sure whether your audience will pay: start with Substack. The free entry point, the built-in discovery, and the simplicity make it the lowest-friction way to test whether your newsletter works before committing to a platform.

If you have a proven newsletter concept, are generating or expect to generate meaningful subscription revenue, and care about owning a content platform that represents your brand properly: Ghost is the right move. You pay a flat fee rather than a percentage, you control everything, and the long-term SEO potential is genuinely better.

Most founders start on Substack and migrate to Ghost once the 10% cut starts to sting. That migration is possible and not catastrophic, but it takes work. If you can see clearly that Ghost is where you will end up, starting there saves you the hassle later.

The Platform Decision Is Not the Hard Part

Founders spend more time comparing platforms than they spend writing their first ten issues. That ratio is backwards.

Your subscribers do not care whether their emails arrive from a Ghost or Substack server. They care whether the content is worth reading and whether you show up consistently. A well-written newsletter on either platform will outperform a generic one on the other.

Choose the platform that matches where you are right now, then direct your energy toward the work that actually compounds: writing something worth reading every single week.
