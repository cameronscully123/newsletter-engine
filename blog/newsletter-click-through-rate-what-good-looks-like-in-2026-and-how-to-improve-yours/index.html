Newsletter Click-Through Rate: What Good Looks Like in 2026 (And How to Improve Yours)

June 16, 2026 · Top of Funnel

Most founders track open rates. They watch that number tick up or down after each send, adjust subject lines, test the preview text, and call it a day.

But open rate tells you one thing: whether someone clicked on your email. It says nothing about whether they read it, trusted it, or did anything because of it.

The metric that actually tells you whether your newsletter is working is your click-through rate.

What Click-Through Rate Actually Measures

Click-through rate (CTR) is the percentage of subscribers who click a link in your newsletter. It is calculated by dividing total clicks by total delivered emails and multiplying by 100.

If 500 people receive your newsletter and 25 click a link, your CTR is 5%.

Unlike open rates, which have been inflated by Apple Mail's automatic pre-loading since iOS 15, clicks are intentional. Someone reads your email, sees a link, and decides to tap it. That is a meaningful signal, and it is one of the most honest measures of audience engagement you have.

What a Good Newsletter CTR Looks Like in 2026

For a founder or agency owner sending to a warm, permission-based list, a CTR between 2% and 5% is solid. Anything above 5% is strong. Anything above 8% is exceptional.

Promotional blasts sent to cold or unclean lists often sit below 1%. That is not a campaign problem; it is a list problem.

The most important thing to understand is that CTR is relative to your own baseline. If your average CTR is 1.8% and you run an issue that hits 3.5%, something worked. Track your trend, not just the number.

Why Most Newsletter CTRs Are Too Low

Low CTR is rarely about the link itself. It is almost always about one of three things.

First, the content does not lead anywhere. Many founders write a newsletter that exists as a standalone piece, with no natural next step for the reader. If there is nothing worth clicking, nobody clicks.

Second, there are too many links. Counterintuitively, packing a newsletter with five or six links typically drives fewer clicks than including one or two clear ones. When you give people too many options, they often choose none.

Third, the call-to-action is too vague. "Read more" or "Learn more" are weak prompts. Readers need to know what they are clicking towards and why it matters to them right now.

How to Write Calls-to-Action That Get Clicked

A good call-to-action tells the reader exactly what happens next and why they should want it. Compare "Click here" to "See how founders are using newsletters to land enterprise clients." One is a direction; the other is a reason.

Make your link text specific. If you are driving people to a case study, say what the case study shows. If you are driving people to a booking page, say what the call is for. Specificity reduces hesitation.

Place your primary link early in the email, not just at the end. Many subscribers read the first few paragraphs and then scan the rest. If your only link is buried in the final line, you are missing the readers who dropped off halfway through.

The Format That Drives More Clicks

Shorter emails typically outperform longer ones on click-through rate. This is not because short emails are better; it is because they focus the reader on fewer decisions.

If you write a 600-word newsletter that builds to one clear link, your reader knows exactly where to go. If you write a 1,500-word essay with three links scattered throughout, the reader has choices to make, and choices slow action.

A useful format is this: one sharp insight, one piece of practical context, and one link that extends the idea. That is a complete email. It also happens to be the format that consistently earns more clicks.

Break your paragraphs often. Dense blocks of text get skimmed. Short paragraphs with white space between them get read. And readers who actually read are far more likely to click.

Consistency Matters More Than Optimisation

The biggest lever on click-through rate over time is not link placement or CTA copy. It is trust.

A subscriber who has opened your last twelve newsletters and found them useful will click links without much prompting. A subscriber who signed up six months ago and has heard from you intermittently will treat your email like a knock at the door from someone they barely recognise.

This is why weekly, consistent sending matters. Every issue you send is another deposit in the trust account you are building with your list. By the time you have something specific to promote or link to, your readers are already primed to respond.

Chasing a higher CTR with tactics while neglecting consistency is treating the symptom rather than the cause. Build the habit of showing up, and the clicks follow.
