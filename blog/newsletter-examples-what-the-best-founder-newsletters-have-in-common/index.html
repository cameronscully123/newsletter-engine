Newsletter Examples: What the Best Founder Newsletters Have in Common

The best way to understand what makes a great founder newsletter is to look at what the best ones are actually doing.

Not the biggest newsletters. Not the media brands. The ones run by individual founders and agency owners that consistently generate leads, referrals, and business opportunities for the people behind them.

They share a set of common characteristics that are worth understanding before you build your own.

They have a point of view, not just a topic

The newsletters people actually read are not defined by their subject matter. They are defined by a specific perspective on that subject matter.

Anyone can write about marketing, or leadership, or agency growth. The newsletters that build real audiences write about those topics with an unmistakable perspective. A belief about how things work. A disagreement with conventional wisdom. A framework the writer actually uses and is willing to defend.

The best founder newsletters take positions. They say things some readers will disagree with. They are not trying to appeal to everyone, because trying to appeal to everyone produces content that resonates with nobody.

The newsletters worth studying are easy to describe in terms of what the writer believes, not just what they write about.

They are written by someone, not produced by a team

The founder newsletters that convert best read like they were written by a human being in the middle of thinking something through.

Not polished. Not corporate. Not produced.

They use the language the writer actually uses in conversation. They include specific details from real situations. They have a sentence or two that feels slightly raw, like the writer is being honest about something they have not fully resolved yet.

That texture is what makes a newsletter feel genuine. And genuineness is what makes readers trust the writer enough to eventually want to work with them.

The newsletters that feel like brand content do not build this kind of trust. The newsletters that feel like a direct message from a smart person who cares about their reader do.

They are consistent to the point of being predictable

The best founder newsletters go out on the same day at roughly the same time every week without exception.

That predictability builds a habit in the reader. Subscribers come to expect it. Some readers actively look forward to it. The consistent cadence is part of what makes the newsletter feel reliable and therefore trustworthy.

The newsletters that go out sporadically, whenever the writer has time, never build this. Readers do not form habits around inconsistent inputs. The relationship stays shallow because the touchpoints are too infrequent to compound.

The founder newsletters that produce the most business results are boring in their consistency. Nothing about the schedule changes. The content is fresh but the rhythm is always the same.

They treat the reader's time with respect

The best founder newsletters are not trying to show how much the writer knows. They are trying to give the reader something useful in the least amount of their time.

That shows up in the writing: short paragraphs, clear structure, no unnecessary preamble. It shows up in the length: most of the best founder newsletters are 400 to 700 words, not 3,000. It shows up in the focus: one idea per issue, developed well, rather than five ideas developed superficially.

The newsletters that lose readers are usually the ones where the writer is writing for themselves rather than the person on the other end. Too long, too detailed, too self-referential.

The newsletters that keep readers come in, make a clear point, and leave. The reader finishes in five minutes and feels like it was worth it.

They connect the content to a business outcome without being salesy

The best founder newsletters are not pitching in every issue. But every issue connects, in some way, to the problem the founder is paid to solve.

This does not require a call to action in every email. It requires that the content itself demonstrate the kind of expertise that makes the reader think: this person knows what they are doing. When I need what they offer, this is who I am calling.

That connection is what separates a founder newsletter that generates business from a founder newsletter that is just a content habit. The content builds the case. The business development happens as a result.
